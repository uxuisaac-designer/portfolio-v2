Klekt was competing in a resale market that was growing faster than its own
browsing experience. People could buy, but only if they already knew what they
were looking for and where to find it.

Rather than one large redesign, we split it into four projects that each
attacked the same problem from a different side: drops pages, brand landing
pages, the site navigation, and the design system underneath all of them.

## Four projects, one problem

Splitting it up was the decision that made the rest possible. A single
“improve the marketplace” project has no edge and no end; four named projects
each have a thing they are for.

They were not equal in size, and they were not independent — the navigation
needed the brand pages to exist before it could point at them, and all of them
needed the design system to stop being a source of inconsistency. Sequencing
mattered more than scope.

## Buying on the day of release

Drops pages let people see what is coming out and when, and sometimes buy or
sell ahead of the official release date.

I validated the idea by interviewing 30 members of the sneaker community, who
said two things clearly: they wanted to be told about upcoming releases, and
they wanted a way to buy before the release date rather than racing everyone
else at the moment it landed.

The analytics agreed from a different direction: logins concentrated on
release days, and the people arriving were coming for one product rather than
to browse. That is what moved drops onto the homepage rather than leaving it as
a section people had to find. If someone arrives on the day for one product,
the shortest path to it belongs on the first screen.

## Brands had no pages

Klekt sold products from over 300 brands and had a page for none of them, and
no all-brands page either. Someone who wanted to browse Nike had nowhere to
go.

The brand pages carry popular items, hot picks, specific models and
information about the brand itself. They gave browsing a destination it had
been missing, and they gave the navigation something worth linking to.

## A menu built around what people search for

The site navigation was the first problem I was handed when I joined, and it
was a menu that listed what the company was organised around rather than what
people were looking for.

I designed a mega menu built from popular searches and popular items, with the
new brand pages and the blog reachable from it. The organising principle
changed from the shape of the catalogue to the shape of the demand.

## A system nobody had followed

Klekt already had a design system. It had aged, and more to the point it had
stopped being followed, so the site was inconsistent in a lot of places — which
is worse than having no system, because it looks like a decision.

Another designer and I rebuilt it for web and app, carrying over the
components that still earned their place rather than starting from nothing.
Doing it alongside the other three projects meant each one shipped against the
new system instead of adding to the drift.

## What I took from it

I joined Klekt as its only designer and left it with the experience of working
alongside another designer, a product team and engineering. It was a steep way
to learn and a fast one, and the contrast between those two situations taught
me more than either would have on its own.

I spent the projects deliberately sharpening the craft — Figma, building user
journeys properly, keeping up with where UI conventions were moving. None of
that arrives as a side effect of shipping. It only happens if you make room
for it.

Talking to the rest of the tech team turned out to be the thing most worth
protecting time for. In a fast startup, some of it remote, there is only so
much time to spend outside your own work — yet engineering and product held
context I could not get anywhere else, and knowing how a decision would land on
someone else's desk made the decisions better.

Working with another designer changed the output rather than just the pace.
Sessions with someone who reads the same problem differently produce things
neither of us would have reached alone, and the design system is better for
having been argued over.
