Klekt sells authenticated sneakers and streetwear, and during the pandemic
resale boom it had one way to buy: pay the asking price. Competitors let people
bid. Klekt had not found a way to build it, and the business forecast bidding
would bring in up to 70% more orders.

I designed the feature end to end — research, flows, and low and high fidelity
screens for app, mobile web and desktop. It launched on the app in May 2022 and
lifted orders by 65–75%, depending on the month. The forecast was close to
right.

## What the competition had worked out

I started with a competitor analysis across nine platforms — StockX, GOAT,
Stadium Goods, eBay, Depop, Grailed, Laced, Wethenew and Kick Game — looking at
how each handled bidding and what else they offered around it.

StockX had solved it best for the same audience as Klekt — a bid and ask
structure that read as a market rather than an auction. But eBay was doing
things StockX wasn’t: messaging between buyer and seller, and a set of offer
mechanics that gave people room to negotiate rather than just to lose.

That mattered for scope. The obvious move was to copy StockX. The more useful
finding was that bidding is one of several ways to let a buyer make the first
move, and the others were cheaper to build.

## Bidding is about not paying full price

I wrote the research questions before doing any of it, so the study had
something to answer rather than a general curiosity to satisfy: how do people
bid now, how do they want to bid, what do they need to know before bidding,
and where does bidding hurt.

Then a survey, plus one-to-one interviews with members of Klekt and Crep
Protect. Five findings came out of it, and they were more specific than I
expected:

Price is the deciding factor in how someone chooses to buy at all. People bid
when they don’t want to pay full price — usually at least £20 off, against an
average item price of £180. Roughly 11%, and specific enough to design a
suggested bid range around. They want to know what a good bid range is before
placing one, because a bid
that is obviously too low is embarrassing rather than strategic. They want
transparency about when payment is actually taken. And they want control over
both the value and the time frame.

I grouped the findings on an affinity map, which settled into seven clusters.

## Mapping every way a bid can end

Working with the product manager, I moved to navigation and flows next, because
a bid is not a screen — it is a state that changes over time and can end in
several ways at once.

I mapped the flows with their edge cases: a bid accepted, expired, outbid,
withdrawn, or beaten while the buyer is mid-checkout. Each of those needed a
place for the user to land and something true to tell them.

I designed it to adapt between app and web from the start, because it was not
settled which would ship.

## Cut to app only

I designed low and high fidelity wireframes for app, mobile web and desktop.
Then the project came under a time constraint and bids were stripped back to an
app-exclusive feature. The mobile web and desktop work was cut.

Designing for adaptability first is what made that survivable — the app
designs were not a rescue job, they were one of three cases I had already
worked through. If I had designed for desktop first and adapted afterwards, the
cut would have cost the whole timeline rather than two of the three surfaces.

## What changed

Bids launched in May 2022, on the app only. Order volume rose 65–75% month to
month against a forecast of up to 70%.

A forecast landing that close usually means the mechanism was understood before
anyone drew a screen. The competitor work had already established that bid and
ask was a proven pattern for this audience rather than a bet, which is the part
of the research that paid for itself.

The £20 finding held: accepted bids clustered around the 11% the research
predicted. What it had not predicted was the tail underneath — a set of buyers
bidding well below that, hunting a bargain rather than negotiating a price.
They were not the people the suggested bid range was designed for, and it did
not stop them.

The flows behaved as mapped. None of the edge cases I had worried about —
expiry, being outbid, a bid beaten mid-checkout — turned into a problem after
launch.

## What I’d do differently

The number I have is order volume. I don’t have bid-to-purchase rate, the
spread on accepted bids, or whether a first-time bidder came back — so I can
tell you bidding worked, but not which part of the design did the work.

The bargain-hunting tail is what I’d design for now. I built a suggested bid
range to stop bids too low to be taken seriously, and a group of buyers ignored
it and bid low anyway. That is a signal about what they came for, not a failure
to read the guidance, and there was probably a product in it.

I’d also push harder on the app-only decision. It was the right call under the
deadline, but I accepted it as a constraint rather than asking what it cost in
buyers who were on desktop — and with the feature performing that well, that
question was worth more than it looked at the time.
