Klekt sells authenticated sneakers and streetwear. During the pandemic resale
boom it had one way to buy: pay the asking price. Competitors let people bid.
Klekt had not found a way to build it, and the business forecast bidding would
bring in up to 70% more orders.

I designed the feature end to end — research, flows, and low and high fidelity
screens for app, mobile web and desktop. It launched on the app in May 2022 and
lifted orders by 65–75%, depending on the month. The forecast was close to
right.

## What competitors solved

I started with a competitor analysis across nine platforms — StockX, GOAT,
Stadium Goods, eBay, Depop, Grailed, Laced, Wethenew and Kick Game — looking at
how each handled bidding and what else they offered around it.

StockX had solved it best for the same audience as Klekt: a bid and ask
structure that read as a market, not an auction. eBay was doing things StockX
wasn’t — messaging between buyer and seller, and offer mechanics that let the
two sides move toward each other.

That mattered for scope. The obvious move was to copy StockX. The more useful
finding was that bidding is one of several ways to let a buyer make the first
move, and the others were cheaper to build.

## Not paying full price

I wrote the research questions before doing any of it, so the study had
something to answer rather than a general curiosity to satisfy: how people bid
now, how they want to bid, what they need to know before bidding, and where
bidding hurts.

Then a survey, and one-to-one interviews with members of Klekt and Crep
Protect. Five findings came out, more specific than I expected.

Price decides how someone buys at all. People bid when they don’t want to pay
full price — usually at least £20 off, against an average item price of £180.
Roughly 11%, and specific enough to design a suggested bid range around.

The rest were about control. Buyers want to know what a good bid range is
before placing one, because a bid that is obviously too low is embarrassing
rather than strategic. They want to know when payment is actually taken. They
want to set both the value and the time frame.

I grouped the findings on an affinity map. It settled into seven clusters.

## Every way a bid ends

Working with the product manager, I moved to flows next. A bid is not a screen.
It is something you leave running, and it can finish while you are not looking.

I mapped the flows with their edge cases: a bid accepted, expired, outbid,
withdrawn, or beaten while the buyer is mid-checkout. Each needed somewhere for
the buyer to land and something true to tell them.

I designed it to adapt between app and web from the start, because it was not
settled which would ship.

## Cut to app only

I designed low and high fidelity wireframes for app, mobile web and desktop.
Then the project came under a time constraint and bids were stripped back to an
app-exclusive feature. The mobile web and desktop work was cut.

Designing for adaptability first is what made that survivable. The app screens
were one of three cases I had already worked through, not a rescue job.
Designing for desktop first would have cost the whole timeline instead of two
surfaces.

## What changed

Bids launched in May 2022, on the app only. Order volume rose 65–75% month to
month, against a forecast of up to 70%.

A forecast landing that close usually means the mechanism was understood before
anyone drew a screen. The competitor work had established bid and ask as a
proven pattern for this audience, not a bet. That is the part of the research
that paid for itself.

The £20 finding held. Accepted bids clustered around the 11% the research
predicted. What it had not predicted was the tail underneath — buyers bidding
well below that, hunting a bargain rather than negotiating a price. The
suggested bid range was not built for them, and it did not stop them.

The flows behaved as mapped. None of the edge cases I had worried about —
expiry, being outbid, a bid beaten mid-checkout — became a problem after
launch.

## What I’d do differently

The number I have is order volume. I don’t have bid-to-purchase rate, the
spread on accepted bids, or whether a first-time bidder came back, so I can
tell you bidding worked without telling you which part of the design did the
work.

The bargain-hunting tail is what I’d design for now. I built a suggested bid
range to stop bids too low to be taken seriously, and a group of buyers ignored
it and bid low anyway. That is a signal about what they came for, not a failure
to read the guidance.

I’d also push harder on the app-only decision. It was the right call under the
deadline, and I accepted it as a constraint rather than asking what it cost in
buyers on desktop. Two of the three surfaces I designed never shipped.
