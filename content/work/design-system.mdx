The design team at Car & Classic designs for two sites: Car & Classic, and
its French counterpart, LesAnciennes. When I joined, the design system under
them was dated, inconsistent and missing parts.

I rebuilt it from scratch. It now runs three themes across web, the app, the
dealer platform and LesAnciennes from around 210 semantic tokens, and it reads
simply enough that both product managers and the CTO design with it.

## Old gaps

There was a design system already. It had fallen behind the UI our competitors
were shipping and behind what we wanted to build. Components were
inconsistent, and some were missing entirely — which we found out one flow at
a time, by designing into the gap.

Every gap cost time on every flow that reached it. LesAnciennes added a second
problem: we designed all its work too, and I wanted a way to bridge the two
systems so one piece of work could serve both sites.

Our head of design and I had been raising a rebuild for a while. Sooner was
cheaper than later.

## Proving the case

A rebuild competes with feature work, so it needed stakeholder sign-off before
it could take a place in the workload. I made the case from inside the old
system.

Its icon set had fewer than 100 icons, inconsistent in size and weight, with
gaps where icons should have been. I replaced it with an organised set from
Font Awesome, in regular and filled. The head of design and I each saved a few
minutes a day with it, which adds up. One corner of the old system, fixed,
showed what fixing the rest would be worth. That got the rebuild prioritised,
and once my workload allowed, I took it on.

## Primitives first

I rebuilt the system rather than patching it, carrying over the parts of the
old one that still held up. Most of the work was mine, with the head of
design contributing.

I started with the primitives: the colours, the token values, and the semantic
layers that sit on top of them. Components came after, built on decisions
already made.

It is atomic — more than 100 components built up from smaller ones — and every
layer of every component is tokenised: colour, spacing, size, border width and
radius.

The developers told us the naming structure they wanted for components, and I
built to it. I improved the documentation for the same reader, so a component
in Figma arrives in Storybook and code already described in their terms.

## One switch, two sites

Between the components and the brands sits a semantic appearance layer of
around 210 tokens, set up as Figma variables. There are three themes: Car &
Classic in light and dark, and LesAnciennes in light.

Changing the variable mode in the appearance panel re-themes anything from a
single component to a whole page. The components are the building and the
theme is the paint. Repainting moves no walls.

We use it on real work, though a design rarely crosses sites untouched. Our SEO
team found that sites which look too alike rank badly, so some designs take
deliberate changes before they go live on the second one.

## Built with agents

I used AI agents while setting the system up. An agent designed the token
structure, and agents helped shape the system as a whole and work out colour
when I added dark mode.

I also wanted the system clear enough for an agent to read, so that AI could
work inside it. It does now. We have agents connected to the system that design
variations of components, pages and flows from its own parts.

## Who uses it now

The system is live across web, the app, the dealer platform and LesAnciennes.
Like any design system, it keeps changing.

It made the team faster. The clearest sign is who can use it: it reads simply
enough that our two product managers and the CTO now create designs with it
themselves.

## What I’d do differently

I would take more time over the set-up. Colour is where I would spend it,
since the palette has to carry two brands and a dark mode, and I would look
for ways to make the system more concise.

Next time I would measure before the system landed and after: how long a flow
takes from brief to handoff, how often components are detached in design
files, and how many questions come back from developers during a build.
