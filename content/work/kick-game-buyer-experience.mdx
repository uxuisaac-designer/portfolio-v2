Kick Game sells rare and collectible sneakers and streetwear — seven stores in
the UK, shipping globally, on roughly 85,000 to 110,000 sessions a month
against a catalogue of about 1,200 products from more than 40 brands. I was
brought in to improve the buying experience across the site.

When I started, complaints about not being able to find a size or a colourway
ran at 20–30 a week. By week 10 after launch they were isolated.

## Two kinds of buyer

Eight moderated, task-based interview sessions told me why people struggled.
Two surveys told me how widely it was felt — 340 responses after purchase, and
210 intercepted mid-session about search and findability.

Both pointed at the same friction. Some buyers arrive knowing exactly what they
want: a specific model, a specific size, often something they’ve been hunting
for months. Others arrive to see what’s landed.

The site served neither well. Search returned poor results for the first group
— only 41% of searches led to a product view. Navigation was unintuitive for
the second. Both failed at the same job from opposite ends.

A catalogue of 1,200 products is not a scale problem. At that size, anything
the shop stocks should be findable.

## What the business wanted

I ran a focus group with the founders and team. Their priorities were
different: the product display page and the checkout.

Where the two inputs met was the interesting part. Customers couldn’t find
things; the business couldn’t convert the people who did. Same funnel,
different halves.

That gave the work two problems rather than six features.

## Finding things

Search and navigation together. The old menu gave every category the same
weight — a wall of identical doors, with nothing to say which one to open. I
rebuilt it around how people actually browse, and gave the sections that
mattered most the room to show it.

## Deciding and buying

The product display page carried the most weight. Higher-quality imagery,
tighter descriptions, and colourways you could switch between without putting
the pair down.

<Figure
  src="/placeholder.png"

/>

Then the size selector, where 5 of the 8 interview participants stalled or
visibly hesitated. It was the clearest single point of friction in the
sessions. Behind it, the checkout: fewer steps, clearer state.

<Figure
  src="/placeholder.png"
  caption="The size selector — the clearest source of hesitation in interviews."
/>

Collection pages were rebuilt alongside: better filtering, clearer
categorisation, a more consistent grid.

## What changed

Search and navigation shipped first, with the PDP and checkout work following
over the next quarter.

The complaint that started the project — customers unable to find pairs they
knew Kick Game stocked — arrived through Trustpilot reviews and support
tickets, and came back second-hand from store staff fielding the same confusion
in person. It ran at 20–30 a week before the work. Within 6 weeks of launch it
was down to 3–8. By week 10 it was isolated rather than routine.

Complaint volume is a proxy, not a funnel. It tells me people stopped
struggling to find things. It doesn’t tell me they bought more.

## What I’d do differently

I have the complaint volume and little else. If I ran this again I’d instrument
zero-result searches, add-to-cart rate and cart-to-purchase before shipping
anything. Then I could tell you what the work did to revenue.

The changes were right and the reasoning held. I can prove half of it, and the
half I can’t is the half a business would ask about first.
