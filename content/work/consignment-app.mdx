Kick Game’s consignors are its supply. They list rare pairs on the platform and
take a cut when they sell — some casually, some as a full income, and the top
10% of them listed half the stock. The app they used to do it was an
afterthought of the buyer experience.

I rebuilt it around what those sellers actually do. Listing a pair went from
around four minutes to around one.

## Our supply was split

Roughly half of our top consignors were dual-listing on competing platforms,
some of them triple-listing. They had not left. They had divided their supply,
which is a different problem and a more recoverable one — you do not win back
someone who has gone, but you can win back share from someone who is still
selling with you.

I interviewed 10 consignors from Kick Game’s own list and ran usability tests
with 5 of them, on our app and on the competitors they were also selling
through.

<Figure
  src="/placeholder.png"
  caption="Interviews and usability tests, ours alongside the platforms they were also selling on."
/>

Testing both was the useful part. Our flows were unclear and the interface
confused people, but that alone doesn’t lose you sellers. What did was the
feature gap — things consignors had elsewhere and couldn’t do here. They
weren’t choosing between platforms on taste. They were choosing on capability.

## Sellers aren’t shoppers

The pattern across the interviews: consignors are operators. They’re tracking
what’s listed, what’s sold, what’s about to expire, and what needs repricing.
That’s a business, and the app was built as though they were browsing.

I mapped the consignor journey as walked rather than as designed, marking
every point where someone gave up and opened a competitor instead. The route
had more exits than destinations.

## A system first

Before any screens, I built the design system and UI library — colour, type,
iconography, interaction patterns, and a full dark mode set.

<Figure
  src="/placeholder.png"
  caption="The UI library: colour, type, iconography and interaction patterns, light and dark."
/>

Doing this first was a decision about pace. I was the only designer on it, and
a component library meant later features cost hours instead of days. Nothing
after this was drawn twice.

## Listing something

Sign-up and sign-in came first, with social logins, since that was the point new
consignors dropped off before ever listing.

Then the sell flow, the thing consignors do most and where the friction was
worst. Faster search, fewer steps, less to think about between having a pair
and having it listed. About four minutes of work down to about one.

Push notifications, so consignors know when something sells or needs attention
without opening the app to check.

Then manage listings — pause and extend. Small features, and the ones that came
up most in interviews. A consignor who’s sold a pair elsewhere needs to pull it
here, immediately.

## What changed

The app launched, and the sell flow did the thing it was built to do: listing a
pair went from around four minutes to around one.

The rest came from the consignors themselves. They told us listing was markedly
easier, and that Kick Game was now competitive with the platforms they had been
favouring — which was the point, since the feature gap was what split their
supply in the first place. We kept our consignors, and sign-up to first listing
rose.

## What I’d do differently

I designed this solo, which meant I could move quickly and it also meant nobody
was checking my reasoning. I’d want a second designer on the flows now, or at
least a structured critique.

Time to list is the number I can put behind the work, and the qualitative
signal ran the same way. What I would add next time is the instrumentation to
size it — listing completion rate, sign-up to first listing, and retention
against the previous quarter — so the result could be stated as precisely as
the problem was.
