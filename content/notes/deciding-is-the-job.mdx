The most expensive decision I have made in the last two years is one I did not
make.

The company I work at now needed a design system. I knew it needed one. I put
it off — not in the way you put off a dentist appointment, but the ordinary
way, where there is always a product deadline in front of it and a design
system is never the thing due on Friday.

What that cost was not a late system. It was everything we shipped in the
meantime, built in a language that did not exist yet.

## An open decision does not sit still

Icons were chosen per project, because there was no library to pull from.
Colour was decided per screen. Type was decided per screen, usually by whoever
happened to be on it. Every one of those was a small local decision made
sensibly by someone doing their job, and the sum of them was a product that did
not look like it came from one company.

That is the part I had wrong. I thought I was deferring a decision. A decision
you have not made does not sit quietly waiting for you — it gets made anyway,
in pieces, by whoever needs an answer that Tuesday. Deferring does not buy you
the decision later. It costs you the ability to make it once.

Importing a full icon library is an afternoon. Settling a colour ramp and a
type scale is a week or two of argument, and both get harder the more screens
depend on them, because by then you are not choosing a scale, you are choosing
a scale and a migration. Doing that early would have given every project after
it something to speak. We would have had better-looking products, sooner, and a
language to build the next thing in.

## The opposite failure is just as real

When time is short I skip the exploring entirely and go straight to designing.
No mapping of the routes a person could take through the thing, no accounting
for the states they arrive in — straight to screens, because screens are
what the deadline asks for.

What comes out is two or three designs that look different and are the same
design. They vary the layout and leave the thinking alone, because the thinking
never happened. Nobody in the review can tell you why they feel samey, and the
reason is that there is only one idea in the room wearing three outfits.

Fast is not the same as good. You cannot rush good design, and near-identical
screens are what rushing actually buys you — not a worse idea, the same idea,
three times.

## Confidence is the stopping rule

Every design I work on goes through at least three rounds of feedback. That is
a floor, not a schedule. The ceiling is a condition rather than a count: we
stop when we have something we are confident to ship to users.

Confidence is a strange thing to build a rule on, because it is also the thing
that breaks the rule.

The person I have most often heard ask for one more round is me. Not always
because the answer is unclear — sometimes I already know which direction is
right and I want it reinforced before I put my name on it. Shipping something
you are unsure about is a horrible feeling, and one more round is a very cheap
way to not have that feeling today.

I do not treat that as a failure of nerve, and I would not tell anyone to stop
doing it. Being reinforced in a decision is worth something real, and the
alternative is worse: a person shipping work they privately doubt, at speed,
is not a person moving quickly. The honest version is that the extra round buys
confidence, confidence is what lets me stop, and I have never found a reliable
way to tell the round that builds confidence from the round that postpones the
moment I need it.

The design system is what that looks like when it runs for a year instead of a
week.

## Knowing which one you are holding

Some decisions decay by staying open. Some decay by closing early. Almost
everything I have got wrong has been mistaking one for the other.

A design system decays open. It is a decision about how everything after it
will look, so every day it stays unmade, more work gets done in its absence and
more of the answer gets set by accident. Making it on incomplete information
beats making it late, because the information you are waiting for is being
produced by the people who need the decision now.

A user flow decays closed. Commit before you understand the routes through it
and you get three variations on your first idea, which is the most expensive
kind of fast — it has the appearance of options and the substance of one.

The two feel identical from the inside. Both present as a reasonable person
wanting to be sure. The tell is what happens while you wait: if other people
are answering the question for you in small pieces, you are holding the first
kind and you have already lost.

I could have told you which kind the design system was at the time. I know
because I was the one saying we needed it.
