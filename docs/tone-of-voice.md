# Tone of voice

The writing standard for everything on this portfolio — case studies now, the
notes later. One source, so the rules cannot drift between places.

Influenced by Benji Taylor's writing: grounded, dry, specific. Confident and
pragmatic, storytelling with rigour under it.

The two case studies to read before writing anything are
`content/work/kick-game-buyer-experience.mdx` and
`content/work/consignment-app.mdx`. Reading them beats reading this file, and
they stay current as the writing evolves.

## Ground interaction explanations in one physical metaphor

When explaining a UI or interaction decision — transitions, navigation, motion,
layout — reach for one grounding physical or spatial metaphor rather than pure
UX vocabulary. "Floats rather than walks." "Flies instead of teleports." "Like
walking through connected rooms."

One per pattern, cleanly. Do not stack two metaphors on the same point. A
different pattern in the same piece can have its own, but keep the images
distinct from each other.

Skip this entirely for factual content — specs, tooling, timelines, sample
sizes.

Worked examples from these case studies:

- The old Klekt menu "listed how the company filed its stock, not what anyone
  came in asking for" — replacing "the organising principle changed from the
  shape of the catalogue to the shape of the demand".
- "If someone comes in on release day for one pair, that pair belongs on the
  first screen they see."
- "Someone who wanted to browse Nike had nowhere to go", extended two
  paragraphs later by "They gave browsing a destination." One image, developed
  — not two competing.

## One or two dry asides per long section, and only in long sections

Drop a short parenthetical or aside at a natural fatigue point — after a dense
explanation, never stacked back to back. Dry and understated, never twee. One
per ~500 words is the ceiling.

**Skip entirely in sections under 300 words.** In practice this rules asides
out of most of this site: case study sections here run 60–200 words, so an
aside reads as padding rather than relief. Do not work around the rule by
splitting a section to qualify.

## Sentence level

- Short declaratives. Cut hedging: "typically", "might", "could potentially",
  "I think", "perhaps", "somewhat".
- Alternate one longer, image-driven sentence with a short flat one. The short
  sentence is the payoff, not the setup. "A single 'improve the marketplace'
  project has no edge and no end. Four named projects each have a thing they
  are for."
- No self-praise adjectives about the work — "innovative", "seamless",
  "delightful", "elegant", "intuitive" — unless the concrete evidence follows
  immediately in the same breath.
- Active voice, first person. "I interviewed 10 consignors", never "user
  interviews were conducted".
- No sentence begins with "And" or "But".
- Show, don't tell. Replace "improved engagement" with the change made and the
  number it moved.

### Banned construction

**"It's not just X, it's Y" / "more than just X".** Never. Say the specific
thing instead.

It hides in paraphrase — "changed the output rather than just the pace" is the
same move wearing different clothes. Deleting the clause usually loses nothing:
"Working with another designer changed the output."

Genuine either/or contrasts are fine and are not this: "carrying over the
components that still earned their place rather than starting from nothing"
weighs two real alternatives.

If a sentence genuinely needs the banned shape, **flag it rather than rewriting
it silently.**

### Endings

No abstract closing statements. Nothing "reflects a commitment to craft" or
"resonates". End a section on a concrete detail or a number.

## Honesty about evidence

The audience is design recruiters, hiring managers and heads of design, and
they read for evidence. A claim with no number is an assertion.

- Never invent a metric, percentage, timeframe or team size.
- Put a statistic in the sentence that makes the claim, not in a stat strip at
  the top. "Listing a pair went from around four minutes to around one" belongs
  in the paragraph about the sell flow.
- Report a range as a range. Complaints falling from 20–30 a week to 3–8 is
  between a 60% and a 90% drop; stating "a 78% reduction" is false precision.
- Keep raw counts raw on small samples. "5 of 8 participants" — 62.5% implies
  precision eight people cannot carry.
- Distinguish a forecast from a result. Quoting a forecast after the fact reads
  as an outcome until somebody asks.
- Separate qualitative from quantitative. Interviews say *why*; surveys and
  analytics say *how often*.
- Where there is no number, say so plainly once and move on — **without
  apologising for it.** "Sign-up to first listing rose" is honest. "I can't
  give you the size of that increase" is a confession, and reads as negative.
  Omission is honest; apology is not more honest, only weaker.

## Structure

- Prose, not bullets. Bullets belong on a CV; this site is a single column and
  lists fight it.
- Lead with impact. The outcome goes in the opening, not three sections down.
- Headings are specific to the story, never generic process labels. "Our supply
  was split", "Not paying full price", "Cut to app only" — not "Research",
  "Ideation", "Solution".
- **Headings stay under about 24 characters.** The contents sidebar sets the
  active label in weight 500, which is wider, so a longer heading rewraps as
  you scroll past it and the list jumps.
- Close reflectively — "What I'd do differently", or similar — and name the
  measurement you would take next time rather than the regret.

## Typography

Typographic apostrophes (’). En dashes in ranges — 2022–2024, 20–30 a week. Em
dashes for asides. Numerals for figures that carry an argument; words for
counts that are prose ("seven stores", "two kinds of buyer"). Never start a
sentence with a digit.

## Notes

Everything above holds for a note unless this section overrides it. Notes are
reflective and editorial where a case study is evidential, and the difference
is smaller than it sounds — the register moves, the discipline does not.

### Carried over without change

Short declaratives. No hedging words. Active voice, first person. No sentence
opens with "And" or "But". No self-praise adjectives. No abstract endings.
Prose, not bullets. Typographic apostrophes, en dashes in ranges, em dashes for
asides.

The banned construction — "it’s not just X, it’s Y", and every paraphrase of it
— stays banned. It gets more tempting in an essay, not less.

### Five deltas

**Asides come off the bench.** The cap is one per ~500 words with a hard floor
at 300, which in practice rules them out of the rest of this site: case study
sections run 60–200 words. Notes clear the floor. The rule has not changed; the
sections finally qualify. Most of a note's extra warmth comes from here, and it
costs no consistency at all.

**A metaphor can carry an essay, not just a pattern.** In a case study the
image explains one interaction and stops. In a note it can be the spine —
introduced early, returned to once, paid off at the end. Still one per idea,
still never stacked.

**Second person is unlocked.** A case study is "I did X, it moved Y". A note
may address the reader directly. This is the largest single lever on warmth,
and it is unavailable in the case studies by nature rather than by rule.

**Reversal counts as evidence.** "I think" stays banned as hedging. "I argued
the opposite of this for two years" is not hedging — it is the strongest thing
an opinion piece has to offer. Named, dated, specific. Uncertain about the
conclusion is allowed; mushy in the sentence is not.

**Evidence changes currency, not rigour.** A case study proves a claim with a
number. A note proves it with a scene: a specific meeting, a specific file, a
specific sentence someone said. **No composite anecdotes.** No "a designer once
told me" if no designer did. That is the essay-writing equivalent of an
invented metric, and it is the likeliest way this writing goes wrong.

### Banned in notes specifically

Clichés a case study never gets near. The dictionary-definition opener. "In
today’s world", "In an age of". The rhetorical-question hook. "Here’s the
thing". LinkedIn cadence — single-sentence paragraphs stacked for drama.
Closing on a call to action.

Chief among them: **"As designers, we…"**. Every one of these topics lists
toward industry-commentary voice, and the collective first person is how a
writer asserts something without owning it. Every claim in a note is one to
stand behind personally.

### Attribution

Anonymised but real. The incidents are true and specific; no employer and no
colleague is named inside a note. "A marketplace I worked at", "the person who
owned the roadmap". The evidence stays concrete enough to carry the argument
without a searchable person reading themselves into it — and the case studies
already name the employers for anyone who wants that context.

### Headings and length

The 24-character limit lifts. It exists only to stop the case-study contents
sidebar rewrapping as a label gains weight 500, and a note has no sidebar. A
note may also run headless where the argument is continuous.

Length is not specified. Read time is computed from the finished text, so
length is a result rather than a target.
